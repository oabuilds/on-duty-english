# 8 Diagnosis Lines That Ward Doctors Use, in Plain English

Eight lines for the moment you have to explain a diagnosis to someone who is frightened. These are not vocabulary words. They are the exact sentences a senior doctor uses, in the order they use them.

## The eight phrases

### 1. Tell me what you have been told so far.
*/tɛl miː wɒt juː hæv biːn təʊld səʊ fɑː/* — finding out where to start
Instead of: Has anyone explained this to you?
Why it works: A yes or no question gets you a yes. This one gets you her words. Now you know where to start, and which words she already has.
Example: Before I start, tell me what you have been told so far.
Tagalog: Sabihin po ninyo sa akin kung ano na ang nasabi sa inyo.

### 2. I am afraid I have some bad news.
*/aɪ æm əˈfreɪd aɪ hæv sʌm bæd njuːz/* — warning before the news
Instead of: So, the results are back.
Why it works: The warning buys you three seconds. In those seconds they stop, and they start listening properly. News with no warning is often not heard at all.
Example: Please, have a seat. I am afraid I have some bad news.
Tagalog: Pasensya na po. May hindi po magandang balita ako.

### 3. Here is what that means for her.
*/hɪər ɪz wɒt ðæt miːnz fɔː hɜː/* — turning the finding into what changes
Instead of: The results indicate a significant abnormality.
Why it works: A name is not news. What changes for her is the news. Say the change first, and the name has somewhere to sit.
Example: That is the medical name. Here is what that means for her this week.
Tagalog: Ito po ang ibig sabihin niyan para sa kanya.

### 4. I am going to stop there for a moment.
*/aɪ æm ˈɡəʊɪŋ tuː stɒp ðeə fɔːr ə ˈməʊmənt/* — leaving a silence on purpose
Instead of: So the next step is a referral, and then we'll book the clinic.
Why it works: After hard news, people stop hearing for a while. Silence is not an empty space. It is the time they need to catch up.
Example: I am going to stop there for a moment. Take your time.
Tagalog: Titigil po muna ako sandali.

### 5. I do not know yet, and I will tell you when I do.
*/aɪ duː nɒt nəʊ jɛt ænd aɪ wɪl tɛl juː wɛn aɪ duː/* — saying you do not know
Instead of: Let's not worry about that yet.
Why it works: Let's not worry sounds like bad news you are hiding. The plain truth is easier to carry. A promise to come back is worth more than a comfortable answer.
Example: I do not know yet, and I will tell you the day I do.
Tagalog: Hindi ko pa po alam, at sasabihin ko po sa inyo kapag alam ko na.

### 6. Which part would you like me to go over again?
*/wɪtʃ pɑːt wʊd juː laɪk miː tuː ɡəʊ ˈəʊvər əˈɡɛn/* — inviting the hard question
Instead of: Do you have any questions?
Why it works: Do you have any questions asks her to admit she did not follow. This one puts the fault on the explanation, not on her. That is the version a frightened person will actually answer.
Example: Which part would you like me to go over again? I can say it a different way.
Tagalog: Aling bahagi po ang gusto ninyong ulitin ko?

### 7. Tell me how you would explain this at home.
*/tɛl miː haʊ juː wʊd ɪkˈspleɪn ðɪs æt həʊm/* — checking they have it
Instead of: Does that make sense?
Why it works: A frightened person nods. A nod is not understanding. Ask them to explain it to someone else, and you hear what they really have.
Example: I want to be sure I have explained this well. Tell me how you would explain it at home tonight.
Tagalog: Sabihin po ninyo kung paano ninyo ito ipapaliwanag sa bahay.

### 8. I am going to write it down, so you have it tonight.
*/aɪ æm ˈɡəʊɪŋ tuː raɪt ɪt daʊn səʊ juː hæv ɪt təˈnaɪt/* — giving them something to keep
Instead of: It'll all be in the letter.
Why it works: The letter comes next week. She has to say this tonight. Written down, the word is hers to keep, and she can show it to whoever asks her.
Example: I am going to write it down, so you have it tonight, with the number to ring.
Tagalog: Isusulat ko po ito para may hawak po kayo mamayang gabi.

## The steps

Find · Warn · Say · Check

Four steps. If you remember nothing else today, remember that order. Someone who is warned, told what it means and then asked to say it back walks out holding the same news you gave them.

## Practise producing it

1. You are about to start. You do not know what she has already been told.
   Answer: Tell me what you have been told so far.
   Why: A yes or no question gets you a yes. This one gets you her words. Now you know where to start, and which words she already has.

2. You have the results in front of you and she is watching your face.
   Answer: I am afraid I have some bad news.
   Why: The warning buys you three seconds. In those seconds they stop, and they start listening properly. News with no warning is often not heard at all.

3. You have said the medical name. It means nothing to her yet.
   Answer: Here is what that means for her.
   Why: A name is not news. What changes for her is the news. Say the change first, and the name has somewhere to sit.

4. You have just said the hard sentence. She has gone completely quiet.
   Answer: I am going to stop there for a moment.
   Why: After hard news, people stop hearing for a while. Silence is not an empty space. It is the time they need to catch up.

5. She asks whether her mother will be all right, and you do not know.
   Answer: I do not know yet, and I will tell you when I do.
   Why: Let's not worry sounds like bad news you are hiding. The plain truth is easier to carry. A promise to come back is worth more than a comfortable answer.

6. She says she has no questions. You can see that she has.
   Answer: Which part would you like me to go over again?
   Why: Do you have any questions asks her to admit she did not follow. This one puts the fault on the explanation, not on her. That is the version a frightened person will actually answer.

7. She is nodding at everything. You need to know what she actually has.
   Answer: Tell me how you would explain this at home.
   Why: A frightened person nods. A nod is not understanding. Ask them to explain it to someone else, and you hear what they really have.

8. She is standing up to leave with one new word to carry.
   Answer: I am going to write it down, so you have it tonight.
   Why: The letter comes next week. She has to say this tonight. Written down, the word is hers to keep, and she can show it to whoever asks her.
