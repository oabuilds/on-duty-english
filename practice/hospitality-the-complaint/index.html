8 Hotel English Phrases That Calm an Angry Guest

Eight phrases for the moment a guest complaint becomes serious. These are not vocabulary words. They are the exact sentences a senior front desk professional uses, in the order they use them.

The eight phrases

Listen, then say each one out loud before you move on.

1/8

That should not have happened.

/ðæt ʃʊd nɒt həv ˈhæpənd/

acknowledgement

instead of I am so sorry, I am so sorry.

Why it works. This agrees with the guest's judgement instead of apologising for it. Repeating sorry puts you below the guest and invites more anger. Agreeing puts you beside them, facing the problem together.

That should not have happened, and I understand why you are upset.

Hindi dapat nangyari iyon.

2/8

Let me see what I can do right now.

/lɛt miː siː wɒt aɪ kən duː raɪt naʊ/

action signal

instead of I will try.

Why it works. Right now is the load-bearing part. I will try sounds like no with extra steps. Naming the moment tells the guest the wait has already started, and that is what stops them repeating themselves.

Let me see what I can do right now, Mister Adeyemi.

Tingnan ko po kung ano ang magagawa ko ngayon din.

3/8

I am going to fix this for you tonight.

/aɪ əm ˈɡəʊɪŋ tuː fɪks ðɪs fɔː juː təˈnaɪt/

commitment

instead of I will try to sort something out.

Why it works. Ownership plus a deadline. I am going to is a promise; I will try to is a hedge. Guests escalate to a manager when nobody has taken ownership, so this sentence is often what keeps the complaint at your desk.

I am going to fix this for you tonight. Give me two minutes.

Aayusin ko po ito para sa inyo ngayong gabi.

4/8

Here is what I can offer you.

/hɪər ɪz wɒt aɪ kən ˈɒfə juː/

pivot

instead of Unfortunately, there is nothing available.

Why it works. This turns the conversation from what went wrong to what happens next. Starting with unfortunately makes the guest brace for a no; starting with here is what makes them listen for the solution.

Here is what I can offer you, and please tell me if it does not work.

Ito po ang maiaalok ko sa inyo.

5/8

I have moved you to a room on the executive floor.

/aɪ həv muːvd juː tuː ə ruːm ɒn ði ɪɡˈzɛkjʊtɪv flɔː/

remedy

instead of I could maybe move you to another room?

Why it works. State the remedy as done, not offered. A question invites negotiation and doubt. The present perfect, I have moved you, tells the guest the problem is already behind them.

I have moved you to a room on the executive floor at no extra charge.

Inilipat ko na po kayo sa isang kuwarto sa executive floor.

6/8

I will take personal responsibility for this.

/aɪ wɪl teɪk ˈpɜːsənəl rɪˌspɒnsəˈbɪləti fɔː ðɪs/

de-escalation

instead of It was the system, not me.

Why it works. Explaining that the booking system failed is true and useless. The guest does not want a cause, they want a person. This sentence is the single most reliable way to stop a guest demanding a manager.

I will take personal responsibility for this until you are settled.

Personal ko pong pananagutan ito.

7/8

Let me get my manager so we can sort this out properly.

/lɛt miː ɡɛt maɪ ˈmænɪdʒə səʊ wiː kən sɔːt ðɪs aʊt ˈprɒpəli/

escalation

instead of I am not allowed to do that.

Why it works. Escalating is not losing. Framed this way it reads as bringing reinforcements for the guest, not hiding behind a rule. Note properly. It promises a better outcome, not just a different person.

Let me get my manager so we can sort this out properly for you.

Tatawagin ko po ang manager para maayos ito nang maayos.

8/8

Is there anything else I can put right for you?

/ɪz ðeər ˈɛniθɪŋ ɛls aɪ kən pʊt raɪt fɔː juː/

closing the loop

instead of Is that okay?

Why it works. Put right is the professional register; fix is fine but blunter. Is that okay invites a grudging yes. Asking what else you can put right signals the standard was missed and you intend to restore it.

Is there anything else I can put right for you this evening?

Mayroon pa po bang iba na maaayos ko para sa inyo?

The four-move order

This is the sequence. It does not change.

→

Acknowledge · Act · Own it · Close the loop

A guest who feels heard, then helped, then owned, almost never asks for your manager.

Now you produce it

Write your answer first, then compare. Retrieving the phrase from a situation is what moves it into the language you can actually use under pressure. There is more than one right answer.

  1. A guest's confirmed room has been given away. They are furious.

    That should not have happened.

    This agrees with the guest's judgement instead of apologising for it. Repeating sorry puts you below the guest and invites more anger. Agreeing puts you beside them, facing the problem together.

  2. A guest is repeating the same complaint for the third time.

    Let me see what I can do right now.

    Right now is the load-bearing part. I will try sounds like no with extra steps. Naming the moment tells the guest the wait has already started, and that is what stops them repeating themselves.

  3. A guest wants to know what you are actually going to do about it.

    I am going to fix this for you tonight.

    Ownership plus a deadline. I am going to is a promise; I will try to is a hedge. Guests escalate to a manager when nobody has taken ownership, so this sentence is often what keeps the complaint at your desk.

  4. You have found a solution and need to move the conversation to it.

    Here is what I can offer you.

    This turns the conversation from what went wrong to what happens next. Starting with unfortunately makes the guest brace for a no; starting with here is what makes them listen for the solution.

  5. You have upgraded them. Tell them in a way that closes the issue.

    I have moved you to a room on the executive floor.

    State the remedy as done, not offered. A question invites negotiation and doubt. The present perfect, I have moved you, tells the guest the problem is already behind them.

  6. The guest is asking for your manager, and you can still handle this.

    I will take personal responsibility for this.

    Explaining that the booking system failed is true and useless. The guest does not want a cause, they want a person. This sentence is the single most reliable way to stop a guest demanding a manager.

  7. You genuinely cannot resolve this at your level.

    Let me get my manager so we can sort this out properly.

    Escalating is not losing. Framed this way it reads as bringing reinforcements for the guest, not hiding behind a rule. Note properly. It promises a better outcome, not just a different person.

  8. The guest has calmed down. Make sure nothing is left unresolved.

    Is there anything else I can put right for you?

    Put right is the professional register; fix is fine but blunter. Is that okay invites a grudging yes. Asking what else you can put right signals the standard was missed and you intend to restore it.
