# 8 English Lines Nurses Use When an On-Call Doctor Says Wait

Eight phrases for the moment you need a colleague to act. These are not vocabulary words. They are the exact sentences a senior nurse uses, in the order they use them.

## What you will be able to do

1. Identify which of two ways of raising a concern actually moves a doctor
2. Explain why “I have a bad feeling about her” loses to a named change
3. Produce all eight lines from the situation alone, without reading them
4. Evaluate an escalation you have just made and name what you would say differently next time

## The situation

It is two in the morning. The patient in bed four is not the patient you took over at ten — her breathing has changed and she is harder to rouse. You have the on-call doctor on the phone, and he has now told you twice that it can wait for the morning round.

## The eight phrases

### 1. I need you to come and see her now.
*/aɪ niːd juː tuː kʌm ænd siː hɜː naʊ/* — making the ask explicit
Instead of: Could you maybe come and have a look at some point?
Why it works: You are asking for one clear thing. A soft question gives the other person somewhere to put it down. When you say what you need, and when, there is nothing to postpone.
Example: I need you to come and see her now, please.
Tagalog: Kailangan ko pong puntahan niyo siya ngayon.

### 2. She has changed since I last saw her.
*/ʃiː hæz ˈtʃeɪndʒd sɪns aɪ læst sɔː hɜː/* — reporting a change, not a number
Instead of: She does not look very good.
Why it works: A change over time is the thing a doctor acts on. Does not look good is your opinion, and it can be argued with. A change is information, and it cannot.
Example: She has changed since I last saw her, about an hour ago.
Tagalog: Nagbago po siya mula nang huli ko siyang makita.

### 3. I am concerned, and this is why.
*/aɪ æm kənˈsɜːnd ænd ðɪs ɪz waɪ/* — naming the worry, then earning it
Instead of: I have a bad feeling about her.
Why it works: Concerned is a word that is heard in hospitals. A bad feeling is not. Saying and this is why forces you to give your reasons, and it tells the other person that reasons are coming.
Example: I am concerned, and this is why. Three things have changed since lunchtime.
Tagalog: Nag-aalala po ako, at ito ang dahilan.

### 4. What would you like me to do while I wait for you?
*/wɒt wʊd juː laɪk miː tuː duː waɪl aɪ weɪt fɔː juː/* — keeping the plan with you
Instead of: So what should I do then?
Why it works: While I wait for you assumes they are coming. You are not arguing about whether they will; you are asking what happens in the meantime. The question quietly settles the first one.
Example: What would you like me to do while I wait for you?
Tagalog: Ano po ang gusto ninyong gawin ko habang hinihintay ko kayo?

### 5. I would like to escalate this.
*/aɪ wʊd laɪk tuː ˈɛskəleɪt ðɪs/* — the formal step, said calmly
Instead of: Fine, I will just call someone else then.
Why it works: This is a normal, expected step, and saying it in the normal words keeps it professional. It is not a threat and it is not a complaint. Everyone in the building knows what it means.
Example: If you are not able to come, I would like to escalate this.
Tagalog: Nais ko po itong iakyat sa mas mataas na antas.

### 6. Can I read this back to you?
*/kæn aɪ riːd ðɪs bæk tuː juː/* — checking you both heard the same thing
Instead of: Okay, got it.
Why it works: Reading back catches the half of the message that did not arrive. It also puts what was agreed into the open, where both of you heard it, which matters later.
Example: Can I read this back to you, so I have it right?
Tagalog: Maaari ko po bang ulitin ito sa inyo?

### 7. I am documenting that I called at this time.
*/aɪ æm ˈdɒkjʊmɛntɪŋ ðæt aɪ kɔːld æt ðɪs taɪm/* — stating the record, not threatening
Instead of: I am writing this down, just so you know.
Why it works: Said flatly, this is simply what you are doing, and it is what you should be doing anyway. Just so you know turns the same sentence into a warning, and the conversation changes.
Example: I am documenting that I called at this time and what we agreed.
Tagalog: Itatala ko po ang oras ng aking pagtawag.

### 8. Thank you. I will call you back if anything changes.
*/θæŋk juː aɪ wɪl kɔːl juː bæk ɪf ˈɛnɪθɪŋ ˈtʃeɪndʒɪz/* — leaving the door open
Instead of: Okay, bye.
Why it works: You end well, and you book the next call before you need it. The second time you ring, you are continuing something, not starting an argument from the beginning.
Example: Thank you. I will call you back if anything changes.
Tagalog: Salamat po. Tatawag po ulit ako kung may magbago.

## The steps

Change · Concern · Ask · Check

Four steps. If you remember nothing else tonight, remember that order. A colleague who hears a change, then a reason, then a clear ask, almost always moves.

## Practise producing it

1. The doctor has said “let me know if she gets worse”. You need them now, not later.
   Answer: I need you to come and see her now.
   Why: You are asking for one clear thing. A soft question gives the other person somewhere to put it down. When you say what you need, and when, there is nothing to postpone.

2. You are opening the call. Give the one fact that makes them listen.
   Answer: She has changed since I last saw her.
   Why: A change over time is the thing a doctor acts on. Does not look good is your opinion, and it can be argued with. A change is information, and it cannot.

3. You have said you are worried and the doctor has not moved. Give them the reasons.
   Answer: I am concerned, and this is why.
   Why: Concerned is a word that is heard in hospitals. A bad feeling is not. Saying and this is why forces you to give your reasons, and it tells the other person that reasons are coming.

4. The doctor is coming but will be twenty minutes. You are alone with her.
   Answer: What would you like me to do while I wait for you?
   Why: While I wait for you assumes they are coming. You are not arguing about whether they will; you are asking what happens in the meantime. The question quietly settles the first one.

5. You have asked twice and been told to wait. This is the formal next step.
   Answer: I would like to escalate this.
   Why: This is a normal, expected step, and saying it in the normal words keeps it professional. It is not a threat and it is not a complaint. Everyone in the building knows what it means.

6. The doctor has given you three instructions quickly and then paused.
   Answer: Can I read this back to you?
   Why: Reading back catches the half of the message that did not arrive. It also puts what was agreed into the open, where both of you heard it, which matters later.

7. The call is ending and you want the time and the decision on the record.
   Answer: I am documenting that I called at this time.
   Why: Said flatly, this is simply what you are doing, and it is what you should be doing anyway. Just so you know turns the same sentence into a warning, and the conversation changes.

8. The doctor is coming. Close the call so that ringing again is easy.
   Answer: Thank you. I will call you back if anything changes.
   Why: You end well, and you book the next call before you need it. The second time you ring, you are continuing something, not starting an argument from the beginning.
